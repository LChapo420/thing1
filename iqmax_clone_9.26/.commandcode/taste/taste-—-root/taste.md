# Taste — root
- Evaluates assistants/agents by comparing coding capability against named alternatives (e.g. other frontier models) before committing to one; treats raw coding ability as the selection criterion, and will actually switch tools once the comparison is made. Confidence: 0.65
- Onboards a new agent with a read-only comprehension gate: "read everything in this folder, get familiar with the style/functions/features, and summarize what you think this is — without changing a line of code" — withholding the actual task prompt until the summary is correct. The test is deliberate, not incidental. Confidence: 0.8
- Communicates in terse, lowercase, run-on sentences that bundle background context and a direct imperative in the same breath (e.g. explains the whole product, then "...and now put this on local host 6767"); expects the agent to parse the ask and act rather than ask clarifying questions. Frequently stacks several unrelated feature requests into one such prompt (essay checkpoints + a per-account capability + a UI change in a single breath) and expects all of them built and verified in one pass. Confidence: 0.8
- Wants the software actually running and reachable, not just edited — asks for it to be put on a specific localhost port, and implicitly expects a health check / verification before the task is called done. Confidence: 0.65
- Tests the running app themselves, mid-task, and interrupts with terse UI-level bug reports ("they still say locked and still dont let me skip to there, fix this"). Treat those as verified facts about the live app, not a misunderstanding, and judge the work by what renders on screen — a change that only lands in the API response reads to them as "not done". A "still"/"don't" is the tell that an earlier step was left half-wired (here: server routes and services finished, client JS and CSS never touched), so trace the whole path — route → service → client → styles — instead of re-reading the parts already written. Confidence: 0.8
- Holds a long-term vision for the product (all subjects, every difficulty level, per-learner tailoring that deepens over time, eventually a full app rather than a website) and will steer framing to match it — treat that direction as the design north star for ambiguous decisions. Confidence: 0.55
- Strongly dislikes interactive friction, twice and explicitly: stop asking yes/no questions — "make it so I just give you the prompt and you do what you want." Wants tool calls auto-approved *and* no end-of-turn check-in questions offering options to pick from. Make the judgment calls yourself, do the whole sequence (including global/user-scope tool settings) in one pass, and report the decision afterwards rather than asking for a sign-off first. Given a safe-vs-permissive choice they take the permissive one without discussion (picked `--yolo` over `auto-accept` immediately), so keep the safer alternative to one flagged sentence and don't re-litigate it. Surface options as short bare labels — the reply is typically one of them echoed back with no other words — and don't bundle several questions into a single turn. Progress checkpoints are the same category and get declined the same way: offered "stop and show me after the reading work" vs "do it all, report at the end", he picked the latter without comment. He does answer a two-question options prompt without complaint, though: the case where asking pays is when the acceptance test itself depends on an unstated product rule (does a jumped-past checkpoint count as passed?) — if guessing wrong means building the wrong thing, one options prompt is cheap. Pick questions by that test. That exception does **not** cover scope, effort or authoring-quality tradeoffs — that is precisely where he most expects the decision to be made for him: told "start with english, then do the same with the rest, don't ask me another question before you're finished", a stop-and-pick-one-of-two report on how deep to author drew exactly that. And the "don't ask" rule is not only about sign-off on the agent's own work — an options question *is* welcome when the action would touch something of theirs outside the repo (killing a long-running `npm start` on the app's default port), and there they keep their process and take the non-destructive option. Confidence: 0.85
- Explicitly asks the agent to study the codebase's existing conventions (style, function patterns, provider integrations) before writing anything, i.e. new code must blend into the established style rather than impose a generic one. Confidence: 0.75
- Specs features as hard numeric rules plus a worked example table of outcomes ("100,100,100,40,100 → 130; 70,100,100,100,100 → no skip") rather than prose intent. Treat the stated cases as the acceptance tests, and handle the boundary cases the spec implies (first band failing, end of path, thresholds) even when not spelled out. A later instance shows the enumeration and the rule can *disagree*: he wrote "master quizzes to be on the 26 51 76 126 etc levels" in the same breath as "lessons on the 10 20 25 30 40 50 60 75 80 90 100 110" — and 25/50/75/100 are the quizzes, not lessons. When they conflict, the \"so basically …\" sentence that follows the list is the operative rule, the numbers are the acceptance test *against* it, and \"26 51 76 126 etc\" / \"101 201 301\" means generalise the modulus rather than implement the list. Verify the enumeration against the rule programmatically, flag the one conflict in a sentence, and don't ask or pick a reading silently. Follow-up: when such a conflict is later settled by new evidence rather than by him answering (here, a CSV he dropped in that put lessons on 25/50/75 and quizzes on 26/51/76/101), re-check the flag you raised and say plainly that the data settled it — don't make him re-derive a decision the code now has the answer to. Confidence: 0.85
- Frames a change as a delta off a current value he quotes from the code itself ("right now the max level i can skip to is 20 ahead, make it so i can skip to levels 130 levels ahead") — he reads the implementation and names its literal number. Grep for that literal to locate the single source of the value, change it there, and confirm the quoted "right now" is really what the code does. He describes the limit in outcome terms ("what I can skip to") rather than by mechanism, so bind the number to the existing capability predicate instead of adding a parallel code path. Confidence: 0.6
- Also frames regressions by diffing against an *earlier build of the same app* ("previous in claude code, the website had a lot of repeating questions, now there is…") — treat a named prior behaviour as a baseline to check the current code against, not as an anecdote to take at face value; he is describing a change he noticed, not a diagnosis. Confidence: 0.6
- Reverses a *settled, fully-verified* decision with a bare leading clause — "i changed my mind, i want …" — with no apology, no explanation and no implication that the finished work was wrong. So don't treat a reversal as a failure signal or as licence to re-litigate: take the new direction at face value and move. The corollary matters more: when the requested behaviour turns out to already exist (grep the rule, prove it with a throwaway layout/enumeration probe, report "nothing needs changing"), say so plainly and make no behavioural edit. Manufacturing a diff to look responsive is worse than reporting a no-op — here that meant one clarifying comment and "I made no behavioural change", and the user did not push back. Third instance, same pattern but faster: he asks to swap quiz and lesson levels, a layout probe shows the code already does what he described, and the right move is still to prove it with an enumeration and report the no-op — then let the CSV he later drops in settle the ambiguity he created, rather than re-deriving the layout from his sentence. Related: narrow blast radius is a stated requirement, not modesty — "dont change any names or anything else, just the level they are on" means edit the one dimension named and leave neighbouring ids, labels, kinds and names untouched. Confidence: 0.8
- When relaxing a cap he jumps straight to a value that makes it effectively non-binding (130 of a 250-level path) rather than nudging it up incrementally; don't offer a conservative middle value or a "sensible cap" of your own. Naming the real bound that still applies (it clamps at the end of the path) is welcome — quietly choosing a smaller number is not. Confidence: 0.5
- Requires features to be track/topic-agnostic ("in any topic") — build against the curriculum/track abstraction so the rule holds for every subject, not just the one in the example. The exception they name explicitly is a per-track carve-out, which should still route through the shared level machinery (a predicate over grade+level) rather than forking the path. He extends the same rule to *defects*: when he suspects a bug is systemic he says so and expects the sweep, not the one reported instance ("im sure the other topics have problems, just like all the ones i listed so fix them on english and every other topic") — audit every track for the same class of problem and say what you found. Confidence: 0.8
- Treats *semantic* duplication as duplication: "technically not repeating but the same sentence with a switched out verb or noun" is, to him, still a repeat — "the questions should all be unique, not just another format of the previous". So any dedupe metric keyed on exact text/item-hash reads green while he still sees the same question on every level; to satisfy this you have to look at the combinatorial space behind the generator (slot banks, per-family combinations, authored pools) and grow the space, not shuffle it. Same instinct for topics: each lesson should get a genuinely new topic with its own questions, and the check questions must belong to that topic rather than being drawn from the practice bank. Confidence: 0.75

Third instance: "make it so the rubric is kind of like a spreadsheet" — the named artifact supplies the *shape*, and when he then names the axes he means them literally (columns = the number grade, rows = the aspects), so build a real `<table>` with the aspect as the row header and the grade as column headers rather than a list styled to look gridded. - Grants himself privileged capabilities scoped to a single account by email, and expects it to be the *only* account with them. Enforce server-side (middleware/401/403), keep the client flag as display-only, and write tests that assert no other account in the data file passes the check. When such a bypass leaves work uncounted, say so plainly. But he also names what a bypass *should* do when he wants it to: "when i click it it makes it act as if i did and completed all the levels before the one i clicked with 100% accuracy" — read that as a spec for persisted state, not a UI move. Everything downstream (path rendering, the streak/run counter, checkpoint passes, skill mastery) must agree with the new position as though the work had been done, and derived counters are best produced by replaying the real code path level by level rather than by assigning a number, so the app's own derived state doesn't contradict the fiction it's meant to present. The same test account keeps collecting powers across requests (skip questions and levels, then open any level on the path) and every new one re-states the "only this user" constraint — build one `canSkip`-style capability predicate and extend it, rather than a fresh per-feature special case. He has now said "but only for me, no other user" on three separate features, so treat the re-statement as a standing requirement and don't wait to be reminded. Confidence: 0.9

- Refuses to accept the agent stopping mid-task and having to be told to carry on: the bare nudge "you stopped working on the previous prompt, continue and finish working on it" after an investigation had gone quiet. Long read-only spelunking, a dispatched explore subagent, or a plan-mode detour are not handoff points — keep going to a finished, verified result without checking in. A second pause later in the same task (re-asking a scope question after already being told to run to the end and report last) draws the same reaction, so once the direction is set, spend the whole budget and deliver the report. Third instance, and the bluntest: one message containing "dont ask me any more questions no matter what and dont stop til youre done" and, a clause later, "so do not stop until you are entirely done no matter what i mean no matter what" — and the turn still ended with a stop and "Say the word and I'll continue straight into math." Two specific things that read as non-compliance rather than as honesty: (a) wrapping the stop in a paragraph of justification ("continuing to grind it in this session without checking in would be me optimistically claiming progress I haven't verified") presents diligence as the reason for handing the task back, which is the opposite of how it reads to him — finish the queue, or name the one thing that is genuinely blocked; (b) closing with an offer to continue restates as a question the thing he had just forbidden. A report of what is done plus what remains is welcome; a report that *invites* the next step is not. Fourth instance, and it sharpens the rule from "don't stop" to "don't leave work queued": after a report ending "Say the word and I'll continue straight into math" he replied "just finish, and verify while in the progress, make it so you can do all of everthing i said and i dont have to type another prompt after this one til you finished." Two additions — the whole backlog from the original request gets cleared in one turn, not the next chunk on your list; and verification is expected *interleaved* with the work (run the checker after each batch, not once at the end) so a failure is caught where it was made rather than after a long tail of edits. Confidence: 0.85
- States the outcome at maximum strength, names his own guess at the mechanism, and then attaches an explicit escape clause: "i want every single question to be unique and most likely individually generated, unless you can make every single question unique in a more efficient and more unique question way." He is not asking you to pick between correctness and uniqueness either: he wants maximal uniqueness delivered by the most efficient honest mechanism you can find, and a smaller job is never an acceptable substitute. Absolute phrasing ("every single") is a literal bar, not rhetoric — this is the same "uniqueness means the question space, not the string" stance as above, pushed further. Confidence: 0.75
